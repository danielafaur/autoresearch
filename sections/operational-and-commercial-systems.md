# Operational and Commercial Crop-Yield Forecasting Systems: Publicly Documented Methodology

This subsection surveys operational and commercial crop-yield forecasting systems and extracts what is publicly documented about their methodology. Consistent with the evidence requirements for this research, claims are limited to what official documentation, institutional publications, or independent accounts confirm. Where a system's internal methodology is proprietary and not publicly disclosed, this is stated explicitly rather than inferred or assumed favorable.

## JRC MARS Bulletin / MARS Crop Yield Forecasting System (MCYFS) — European Commission Joint Research Centre

The MARS (Monitoring Agricultural Resources) Bulletin is an operational EU institutional system, first published in 1992, that provides monthly crop growing-condition assessments and quantitative crop yield forecasts for EU Member States and neighbouring countries [1]. It directly implements Article 22 of Regulation (EU) 1306/2013 on the monitoring of agricultural resources and is described by the Joint Research Centre (JRC) as a critical service supporting EU crisis-management and market-transparency tools [1].

According to the JRC's own description, the yield forecasts published in the Bulletin are "mainly based on analysis of weather-driven crop model results and Earth observation data" [1]. This confirms that MCYFS combines (a) meteorologically driven crop growth models and (b) remote sensing indicators, but the publicly accessible overview page does not name the specific crop growth model(s) used, does not disclose the satellite platforms or spectral indices ingested, and does not state spatial or temporal resolution, prediction lead times relative to harvest, or accuracy metrics (R2, RMSE, or equivalent) [1]. Underlying maps, graphs and time series are made available through the associated Agri4Cast Toolbox, and bulletin archives are available from 2007 onward, but a full technical methodology (e.g., model equations, calibration procedure, validation statistics) was not found in the publicly accessible institutional page consulted [1].

**Relevance to OGOR/HARVEST:** MCYFS is the longest-running operational EU system combining crop models and EO data for yield forecasting, and is the natural institutional benchmark for any EU-based yield forecasting claim. However, based on what is publicly disclosed, MCYFS forecasts are described as being generated from crop-model outputs conditioned on weather and EO observations up to the current date — corresponding to methodological category 2 (partial EO plus meteorological/crop-model information) as defined in the HARVEST research goal, not category 4 (explicit forecasting/reconstruction of the future EO trajectory followed by yield prediction from the completed trajectory). No evidence was found in the sources reviewed that MCYFS explicitly forecasts or reconstructs the unobserved future part of the vegetation trajectory before deriving a yield estimate.

**Confidence: MEDIUM | Depth: LOW**

**Uncertainty:** The publicly accessible JRC page provides only a high-level description. A more detailed methodological picture (e.g., confirmation of the WOFOST crop growth model, which is associated with MARS in secondary literature) would require access to peer-reviewed MARS methodology papers; a ScienceDirect article addressing this could not be retrieved (HTTP 403) during this research pass, so its content is not cited here. This should be revisited with direct access to JRC technical reports or the Agri4Cast documentation before finalizing any claim about the specific crop model used.

## GEOGLAM Crop Monitor — Group on Earth Observations Global Agricultural Monitoring Initiative

The GEOGLAM Crop Monitor is an international, multi-agency operational system; its Crop Monitor for Early Warning (CM4EW) component, created in 2016, produces monthly consensus assessments of expected crop-condition and yield outcomes for countries at risk of food insecurity [2][3]. It is coordinated by researchers at the University of Maryland and NASA Harvest, with NASA Harvest funding the activity as part of the US contribution to GEOGLAM [3].

The Crop Monitor's own published methodology describes a **consensus-based process** rather than a single automated forecasting algorithm [2]. Partner organizations (national, regional and global monitoring agencies, space agencies, and universities) each submit independent crop-condition classifications through a shared web-based tool; the Crop Monitor secretariat compiles these into a draft report; EO datasets are compared against the submitted assessments to flag discrepancies; and a video-conference is held among the international partners to reach consensus before publication, on a fixed monthly schedule (the first Thursday of each month, eleven times per year) [2]. The methodology page confirms that EO data products, crop masks, and crop calendars are used as inputs, and separately, GEOGLAM materials state that EO data and machine learning are used more broadly across the initiative for crop condition assessment and yield forecasting [3], but the specific machine-learning algorithms, spatial/temporal resolution, or quantitative accuracy of the consensus product are not disclosed in the pages reviewed.

**Relevance to OGOR/HARVEST:** GEOGLAM Crop Monitor is an expert-consensus early-warning product rather than a per-field statistical or machine-learning yield model, and it does not claim to forecast or reconstruct future vegetation trajectories; it aggregates independent assessments of currently observed conditions. It is therefore not close prior art for the HARVEST category-4 pipeline, but it is a relevant institutional benchmark for how "early-season" monitoring is currently operationalized internationally without explicit trajectory forecasting.

**Confidence: MEDIUM | Depth: LOW**

## Planet (PlanetScope) + Copernicus Sentinel-2 — Smallholder Maize Yield Estimation, Malawi (ESA-documented collaboration)

ESA's Earth Online news channel documents a project using near-daily PlanetScope imagery combined with Copernicus Sentinel-2 data to estimate maize yield on smallholder farms in Malawi [4]. According to this ESA account, PlanetScope's higher spatial resolution was used specifically to address the limitation that smallholder fields are small and intermixed with non-crop area, a scale at which Sentinel-2 alone was judged insufficient [4]. The approach combined satellite-derived indicators with crop and hydrology models and ground-based field verification (measuring crop size, biomass and harvest index) to produce a statistical yield estimate [4]. The reported accuracy, per the ESA account, was "up to 60% level of confidence in the accuracy of predicting crop yields across smallholder farms" [4] — a figure that is stated without an accompanying definition of the confidence metric, so it should not be equated with a standard R2 or RMSE value without further methodological detail. The account describes the project as intended to feed into agricultural monitoring and intervention programmes in Malawi, but does not document a transition to sustained operational deployment, so it is best characterized as a research/pilot-stage application rather than a mature operational system [4].

**Relevance to OGOR/HARVEST:** this is a documented example of combining a high-revisit commercial constellation (Planet) with Sentinel-2 to compensate for spatial and temporal observation gaps in smallholder contexts. It addresses incomplete *spatial* sampling, not the *temporal* completeness problem HARVEST targets (forecasting the unobserved future part of the seasonal trajectory). No evidence in this source indicates that future vegetation trajectories were forecast or reconstructed before yield estimation; the method uses only observed imagery combined with ground and model data, placing it in methodological category 2, not category 4.

**Confidence: MEDIUM | Depth: LOW**

## Gro Intelligence — US Corn and Soybean Yield Forecast Models

Gro Intelligence has self-published a technical description of its yield forecasting methodology, first released in April 2017 for US corn and subsequently extended to soybean and other crops/geographies [6]. According to this account, written by the company's founder/CEO, the model architecture is a **meta-model that assigns an agronomically-informed weighting scheme across a suite of machine-learning sub-models**, rather than a single algorithm [6]. The model was trained on verified historical yield records for approximately 1,500 US growing counties, using USDA data spanning 2001–2016 [6].

Publicly disclosed input data include satellite-derived evapotranspiration measurements, land surface temperature, satellite-based crop-health/vegetation indicators, weather data, and economic indicators, combined with pixel-level satellite coverage described as spanning "every field in every county" rather than sample-based survey plots [6]. Spatially, forecasts are produced and aggregated at county and national level; temporally, forecasts are automatically regenerated at least every eight days, with updates described as weekly during the growing season [6]. Gro reports concrete quantitative backtested accuracy: an average error of 2.69% by mid-August across 2001–2015 backtests, an average error of 2.44% by mid-August across 2001–2016 backtests, and a live 2016 forecast that came within 1.40% of the final USDA yield figure by the end of August [6]. A dedicated public model page for the US corn model exists on the company's site, confirming the model's public availability, though its full page content could not be independently re-verified in this pass because of server rate-limiting (HTTP 429) [7].

**Relevance to OGOR/HARVEST:** Gro's own description characterizes the model as continuously ingesting and re-weighting *currently observed* satellite, weather and soil signals as the season progresses — i.e., an in-season, updating multimodal yield estimator. This corresponds to methodological category 2 (partial EO plus meteorological/other auxiliary information) as defined in the HARVEST research goal. No description of an explicit step forecasting or reconstructing the unobserved future part of the vegetation trajectory was found in the source reviewed; the model instead re-estimates yield directly from observations available up to each update date, with accuracy improving as the season advances (consistent with reported error shrinking from backtest averages toward the 1.40% live figure by late August, close to harvest). This is a documented example of the "accuracy improves as more of the season is observed" pattern relevant to HARVEST's central premise, but it is not evidence of a category-4 pipeline.

**Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH | Depth: MEDIUM**

**Uncertainty:** The primary source [6] is self-published by the company (a LinkedIn post by the founder), not an independent peer-reviewed validation, so the reported accuracy figures should be treated as company-reported rather than independently verified. The exact machine-learning sub-model types (e.g., regression, tree-based, neural) are not named in the source.

## Descartes Labs — Corn and Soybean Forecasting Platform

Descartes Labs has published company blog posts describing an in-season corn and soybean yield forecasting capability built on what it calls the "Descartes Labs Forecasting Platform," which combines multi-petabyte satellite imagery and weather data processed with machine learning [8][9]. Per this account, the platform is designed to survey fields at daily cadence and to produce weekly, in-season, county-level yield forecasts throughout the growing season [8]. The company reports that its publicly released 2015 and 2016 corn forecasts came within one bushel of the eventual official USDA yield figure [8].

Direct retrieval of the full company blog posts was blocked (HTTP 403) during this research pass, so the details above are drawn from search-index snippets of the pages rather than a fully verified full-text read, and are reported here with that explicit caveat rather than treated as independently confirmed [8][9]. No specific algorithm family (e.g., CNN, gradient-boosted trees, or other) is named in the retrievable material, and no independently published accuracy metric (R2, RMSE/MAE) beyond the "within one bushel" company claim was found.

**Relevance to OGOR/HARVEST:** As with Gro Intelligence, the disclosed elements describe a system that re-estimates yield from satellite and weather observations available up to the current forecast date, updated weekly as the season progresses — methodological category 1–2, not category 4. No evidence of explicit future-trajectory forecasting or reconstruction was found.

**Confidence: LOW-MEDIUM | Depth: LOW**

**Uncertainty:** This entry should be revisited with direct access to the Descartes Labs posts (e.g., via an alternative retrieval method) or any peer-reviewed validation of the platform, since the current evidence is search-snippet-derived rather than directly verified full-text.

## Platforms Where Methodology Remains Undisclosed: aWhere and EUMETSAT

aWhere and EUMETSAT were also searched for publicly available technical documentation of a yield-forecasting methodology specifically. No company technical whitepaper, peer-reviewed independent validation study, or conference paper disclosing aWhere's internal yield-forecasting algorithms, training data, resolution, or accuracy metrics was located in this research pass. EUMETSAT's role, based on the sources reviewed, is primarily as a satellite data and meteorological product provider (e.g., inputs consumed by systems such as MCYFS) rather than as a publisher of its own end-to-end yield-forecasting methodology, and no EUMETSAT-specific yield-forecasting methodology document was found [1]. Consistent with the requirement to avoid unsupported promotional claims, no accuracy, novelty, or methodological claim is made here about either organization beyond noting that a yield-forecasting methodology is not publicly disclosed in the sources reviewed.

**Confidence: LOW | Depth: LOW**

**Uncertainty:** This is a negative finding (absence of public documentation), not confirmation that these organizations lack a rigorous methodology internally (for aWhere) or do not contribute to yield forecasting indirectly (for EUMETSAT, as a data provider). It should not be used to support or deny any novelty claim about HARVEST relative to these specific systems. Direct outreach, patent-literature search, or access to any gated technical documentation would be needed to close this gap; this was outside the scope of the web sources accessible in this research pass.

## Summary Relative to the Four HARVEST Methodological Categories

Of the systems with publicly documented methodology reviewed here, all identified operational examples (MCYFS, GEOGLAM Crop Monitor, the Planet/Sentinel-2 Malawi maize project, Gro Intelligence's US corn/soybean models, and Descartes Labs' forecasting platform) rely on EO and/or crop-model/weather/soil information available up to the assessment date, re-estimating yield as that observation window grows — corresponding to methodological categories 1–2. No system reviewed in this research pass was found to publicly document an explicit step in which the future, unobserved part of the seasonal vegetation trajectory is forecast or reconstructed and then used as an input to yield prediction (category 4). This is consistent with, but does not by itself prove, a novelty gap for HARVEST: the remaining commercial platform with undisclosed methodology (aWhere), the parts of Descartes Labs' methodology not independently verifiable in this pass (algorithm family, full technical detail), and the possibility of undisclosed proprietary steps within Gro Intelligence's or Descartes Labs' pipelines remain open areas where a category-4 pipeline cannot be fully ruled out. This gap should be flagged explicitly in the funding-proposal novelty section rather than treated as evidence of absence.
