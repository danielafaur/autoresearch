# Task 5 — Existing Commercial and Research Systems Combining EO, Phenology, Anomaly/Risk Detection, Explainability, and Human-Readable Outputs; the SEIRA Gap

This section surveys operational commercial crop-monitoring platforms and research/institutional systems to determine whether any existing system already combines all five components SEIRA targets — (a) EO time series analysis, (b) crop phenology extraction, (c) anomaly/stress/risk detection relative to expected trajectories, (d) explainable/interpretable evidence for detected anomalies, and (e) automated human-readable descriptions or risk narratives — and to identify the closest prior art and the precise remaining gap.

## Commercial platforms: EO, phenology and anomaly detection are mature; explainability and automated narrative generation are not

EOS Data Analytics' EOSDA Crop Monitoring platform is a representative, well-documented example of the current state of commercial crop-monitoring software. Per the company's own product page, it ingests Sentinel-2 and PlanetScope imagery cleared of clouds and shadows, provides a "Growth stages" feature that tracks crop development phase on a BBCH scale from the sowing date, and provides a "Risk Map" feature that monitors "the risk of diseases, weather, and vegetation indices change" across fields for early threat detection [34]. This confirms that at least one leading commercial platform already operationalizes EO time series ingestion (a), phenology tracking (b), and anomaly/risk flagging (c) as integrated, field-level product features [34]. However, the same product documentation shows no evidence of an explainable-AI component (e.g., SHAP-style feature attribution, or any stated method for explaining *why* a pixel or field was flagged as anomalous) [34]. The platform's "Automated Reports" feature generates customizable reports on crop performance, but based on the available product description these are structured/templated reports built around maps and charts rather than free-text, AI-generated narrative explanations or risk descriptions grounded in the specific detected anomaly [34]. This pattern — mature EO ingestion, phenology and anomaly detection, but no disclosed explainability method and no generative narrative output — is consistent with the broader commercial landscape found in this research pass; no leading commercial platform reviewed (EOSDA being the most thoroughly documented) was found to publicly disclose both an interpretability method for its anomaly/risk flags and an automated human-readable narrative-generation capability.

**Confidence: MEDIUM | Depth: MEDIUM**

**Uncertainty:** This assessment is based on the publicly accessible marketing/product page for EOSDA Crop Monitoring [34], not on technical documentation, a peer-reviewed methodology paper, or direct product testing; it is possible proprietary explainability logic exists internally without being disclosed publicly. Other commercial platforms mentioned in preliminary search results (e.g., "yieldsApp," described in aggregated search snippets as combining phenology tracking, anomaly detection and "multi-season field interpretation") could not be independently verified via a primary source in this research pass and are therefore not cited as confirmed evidence.

## Institutional/research early-warning systems: ASAP is the closest documented prior art, but its "explainability" and "narrative" are human-expert, not AI-generated

The strongest documented example of a phenology-aware anomaly/risk-detection system with a human-readable narrative output is ASAP (Anomaly hot Spots of Agricultural Production), an operational global early-warning system developed by the European Commission's Joint Research Centre and described in a peer-reviewed paper by Rembold et al. (2019) in *Agricultural Systems* [33]. ASAP combines MetOp/AVHRR NDVI time series (1 km resolution, since 2007), ECMWF-derived rainfall anomalies (Standardized Precipitation Index), and satellite-derived vegetation phenology (computed from SPOT-VEGETATION archives to determine growing-season timing and crop development stage) [33]. Its anomaly-detection step is phenology-aware in exactly the sense relevant to SEIRA: NDVI and rainfall anomalies are evaluated relative to the expected phenological stage of the growing season, with automated warnings triggered when standardized anomalies exceed a -1 standard-deviation threshold over a minimum share (≥25%) of active cropland/rangeland in an administrative unit [33]. Crucially, ASAP does not stop at automated flags: a two-step framework combines the automated warnings with a monthly expert-analyst review that produces country-level "hotspot" assessments, statistics, temporal-profile graphs, and short narrative summaries, published through an interactive public webGIS portal [33].

ASAP is therefore the closest documented prior art to the full SEIRA chain identified in this research pass: it demonstrably links EO time series (a), explicit phenological referencing (b), and threshold-based anomaly detection relative to expected/historical trajectories (c) into an operational, published pipeline. However, two of the five SEIRA components are met only in a pre-AI, human-mediated form rather than through automated, learned methods. First, ASAP's "explainability" is not model-based interpretability (e.g., attention maps, SHAP values, or counterfactual evidence linking a specific anomaly to specific input features) — the anomaly-detection step is a transparent, rule-based statistical threshold (SPI/NDVI standard-deviation cut-offs), which is inherently interpretable but is not the kind of learned-model explainability SEIRA targets for more complex, ML-driven anomaly detectors [33]. Second, ASAP's narrative summaries are written by human expert analysts during a monthly review meeting, not generated automatically by a language model from the underlying EO/anomaly evidence [33]. ASAP thus establishes that the *problem framing* SEIRA addresses (phenology-referenced anomaly detection feeding into human-readable risk communication) is operationally valuable and already deployed at global scale, but it does not close the AI-explainability or automated-narrative-generation gap.

**Confidence: HIGH | Depth: HIGH**

## No system found that automates both explainability and human-readable narrative generation on top of EO-based anomaly detection

A review-level source, Zhu, Qin, Su, Lin, Li and Gao, "Harnessing large vision and language models in agriculture: a review" (*Frontiers in Plant Science*, 2025) [35], provides independent, peer-reviewed corroboration that the automated explainability-plus-narrative-generation layer SEIRA targets does not yet exist as an integrated system in agriculture. The review reports that only about 0.7% of AI papers on arXiv address agriculture, and that existing large vision-language and language-model applications in the domain remain "mostly limited to QA tasks" over text-image pairs (e.g., crop-disease visual question answering), rather than end-to-end pipelines that take EO time series as input and produce risk narratives as output [35]. The review explicitly identifies the absence of "truly multimodal systems integrating diverse data sources for comprehensive farm monitoring and decision support" as a key open gap, alongside insufficient domain-specific agricultural datasets and high computational infrastructure demands [35]. This is consistent with what the commercial-platform review above found directly (EOSDA: EO, phenology and anomaly detection without disclosed explainability or narrative generation) and with what the institutional-system review found (ASAP: phenology-aware anomaly detection with human-, not AI-, generated narratives) [33][34][35]. No source located in this research pass — commercial, institutional, or peer-reviewed research literature — documents a single system that (i) ingests EO time series, (ii) extracts or references crop phenology, (iii) detects anomalies relative to expected/historical trajectories, (iv) provides model-based interpretable evidence for those detections, and (v) automatically generates human-readable descriptions or risk narratives from that evidence.

**Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH | Depth: MEDIUM**

**Uncertainty:** This is a negative finding (absence of evidence for an integrated system), based on a limited number of searches and a review article with its own literature-coverage limits, not an exhaustive survey of all commercial and research systems worldwide. It is possible that a proprietary commercial system (e.g., one of the many precision-agriculture platforms not directly reviewed here, such as Regrow/Cropio, Satelligence, or aWhere) has undisclosed internal capabilities matching some or all of the missing components; absence of public documentation is not proof of absence of capability.

## Precise SEIRA gap relative to the closest prior art

Relative to the strongest identified prior art (ASAP [33] for the EO–phenology–anomaly chain, and EOSDA Crop Monitoring [34] for the commercial operational equivalent), the precise gap SEIRA addresses is: replacing (i) rule-based, threshold-only anomaly detection and (ii) human-expert-written narrative summaries with (i) a learned anomaly/stress-detection method that provides model-based interpretable evidence (not just a pass/fail statistical threshold) and (ii) automated, AI-generated human-readable risk narratives grounded in that interpretable evidence — while retaining the phenology-referenced comparison-to-expected-trajectory framing that ASAP already validates as operationally meaningful at scale [33][34][35]. No system reviewed in this research pass closes both halves of this gap simultaneously.

**Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH | Depth: MEDIUM**
