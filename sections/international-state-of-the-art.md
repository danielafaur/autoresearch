# International State of the Art

## Benchmark Studies Comparing Sentinel-2 Time-Series Yield Models

### Systematic reviews synthesizing the field

A 2026 systematic review by Narimani, Pourreza, Moghimi and Farajpoor, "Sentinel-2 for Crop Yield Estimation: A Systematic Review" (arXiv:2603.23779, submitted 24 March 2026), synthesizes recent advances in Sentinel-2-based crop yield estimation and is the most directly on-point review identified for the HARVEST state of the art [10]. It identifies a shift from regional to field- and sub-field-scale yield assessment driven by three main methodological families: (i) empirical models combining vegetation indices with machine learning and deep learning (Random Forest, CNNs); (ii) data assimilation of Sentinel-2-derived biophysical variables (notably Leaf Area Index) into process-based crop growth models such as WOFOST and SAFY; and (iii) data fusion of Sentinel-2 optical data with Sentinel-1 SAR to mitigate cloud-related gaps in the optical time series [10]. The review concludes that ML/DL and hybrid frameworks can explain substantial within-field yield variability, but that performance is constrained by limited ground-truth data, cloud-induced gaps in the observed time series, and poor model transferability across years and locations — and it explicitly names "improved in-season observations" as a future research direction, which is directly relevant to HARVEST's premise that the unobserved future part of the season limits early prediction [10]. The review abstract text was retrieved directly; it does not, in the retrieved excerpt, enumerate the specific number of studies reviewed, per-crop breakdowns, or quantitative R²/RMSE ranges, so those details cannot be reported here.

**Confidence: HIGH | Depth: MEDIUM**

### Cross-modal and cross-model benchmark studies

Pathak et al. (Deepak Pathak, Miro Miranda, Francisco Mena, Cristhian Sanchez, Patrick Helber, Benjamin Bischke, Peter Habelitz, Hiba Najjar, Jayanth Siddamsetty, Diego Arenas, Michaela Vollmer, Marcela Charfuelan, Marlon Nuske, Andreas Dengel), "Predicting Crop Yield With Machine Learning: An Extensive Analysis Of Input Modalities And Models On a Field and sub-field Level," presented at IEEE IGARSS 2023 (arXiv:2308.08948, submitted 17 August 2023), is a peer-reviewed benchmark study that compares crop- and model-agnostic machine learning methods for sub-field-level yield prediction using Sentinel-2 imagery as the primary input modality, combined with weather, soil, and digital elevation model (DEM) data in an early-fusion framework designed for global scalability [11]. Its central finding is that the best-performing combination of input modalities is not universal but depends on region, crop, and the specific model used — directly supporting the HARVEST proposal's framing that EO-only versus EO-plus-auxiliary-data approaches (methodological categories 1 and 2 in the HARVEST comparison) trade off differently across contexts [11]. The retrieved abstract does not report specific crop types, R²/RMSE/MAE values, or spatial/temporal resolution figures, so quantitative performance cannot be cited from this source at this time.

**Confidence: HIGH | Depth: MEDIUM**
**Uncertainty:** Quantitative accuracy metrics (R², RMSE, MAE) for [11] were not present in the retrieved abstract; the full paper would need to be consulted for category-1-vs-category-2 performance deltas.

A field-scale benchmark study, "Winter wheat yield estimation at the field scale using sentinel-2 data and deep learning" (Computers and Electronics in Agriculture, 2023, DOI 10.1016/j.compag.2023.108555), compared Random Forest (RF), a standard Convolutional Neural Network (CNN), and an attention-based 1D CNN ("ACNN") for estimating winter wheat yield at county and field scale in Henan Province, China, using Sentinel-2 MSI Level-2A time series [12]. According to search-indexed snippets of the paper (direct WebFetch of the ScienceDirect page returned HTTP 403 during this research pass, so this is not a full-text-verified read), the ACNN model achieved R² = 0.44 and RMSE = 1544 kg/ha, using attention weighting over convolutional features to reduce high-yield underestimation and low-yield overestimation bias relative to RF and plain CNN baselines [12]. The study reportedly identifies the heading stage as the pivotal phenological period for yield formation and highlights spectral bands B8 (835.1 nm, NIR) and B12 (2202.4 nm, SWIR) as the most informative for yield estimation [12]. This is a useful data point for the HARVEST proposal because it quantifies a modest R² (0.44) for a purely observational, non-forecasting (category-1) deep learning approach on a single crop, which HARVEST can use as a comparison baseline for category-4 pipelines — but it should be treated as provisional pending direct access to the full text.

**Confidence: MEDIUM | Depth: LOW**
**Uncertainty:** This paper's methodology and metrics were extracted from search-engine snippets, not from direct retrieval of the article. The R²=0.44 / RMSE=1544 kg/ha figures and the "heading stage" / B8-B12 band findings should be independently verified against the published PDF before being used as a precise quantitative baseline in the funding proposal.

### Synthesis on crop coverage and methodology comparisons

Taken together, the identified benchmark and review literature confirms three consistent patterns relevant to HARVEST. First, systematic reviews (e.g., [10]) converge on a taxonomy of three non-forecasting methodological families for Sentinel-2 yield estimation — vegetation-index/ML-DL empirical models, crop-growth-model data assimilation, and Sentinel-1/Sentinel-2 data fusion — none of which explicitly forecast or reconstruct the unobserved future portion of the vegetation trajectory before predicting yield; this is consistent with, but not a substitute for, a dedicated search for category-4 (forecast-then-predict) pipelines, which should be pursued separately. Second, cross-modal benchmarking work such as [11] shows that reported performance is highly context-dependent (region, crop, model), which cautions against citing any single R²/RMSE figure as representative of "the" state of the art. Third, single-crop field-scale deep learning benchmarks such as [12] report only moderate explained variance (R² in the 0.4–0.5 range) even with attention-based deep learning on relatively rich Sentinel-2 time series, suggesting headroom that HARVEST's future-trajectory-forecasting approach could plausibly address — though this inference is not itself evidence of novelty and must be corroborated by a dedicated search for category-3/category-4 prior art.

**Confidence: MEDIUM | Depth: MEDIUM**
**Uncertainty:** None of the sources gathered in this pass directly addresses methodological category 3 (forecasting/reconstructing the future EO trajectory) or category 4 (the full HARVEST-type pipeline). This gap should be treated as "not yet searched" rather than "confirmed absent," and a targeted follow-up search on vegetation-index/EO trajectory forecasting (e.g., LSTM/Transformer/Gaussian Process extrapolation of NDVI or biophysical variable time series) is still required before any novelty claim for HARVEST can be supported.

**Coverage gap — crop, geography, and quantitative comparison.** The evidence base assembled in this pass is narrower than the section title implies. Of the four benchmark/review sources, only [12] reports quantitative accuracy figures (R², RMSE) for a direct multi-model comparison, and it is the lowest-confidence source in the set (Tier 3, snippet-only, unverified full text) and covers a single crop (winter wheat) in a single region (Henan, China). [10] and [11] are Tier 1 but their retrieved abstracts contain no per-crop or per-region quantitative breakdowns, and [13] is unverified beyond its title. No source in this pass benchmarks Sentinel-2 time-series models for corn, soybean, or rice, nor covers North America, Europe, or Africa with quantitative model-comparison data. Consequently, no cross-study quantitative comparison table (e.g., RF vs. LSTM vs. Transformer R²/RMSE by crop) can yet be constructed, and any claim about "typical" Sentinel-2 yield-model accuracy should be treated as unsupported until additional crop- and region-diverse benchmark studies with verified full-text access are located.

## Deep Learning Architectures for End-of-Season Yield Prediction from Full-Season EO Time Series

This subsection surveys CNN, LSTM, hybrid CNN-LSTM, and transformer architectures that ingest a (near-)complete season of EO time series to predict yield — i.e., methodological category 1 in the HARVEST comparison (direct yield prediction from an observed trajectory), here evaluated at, or near, full-season completeness rather than mid-season. These architectures represent the "final yield prediction from a trajectory" component that a HARVEST category-4 pipeline would need downstream of a future-trajectory-forecasting stage.

### CNN-LSTM as the dominant hybrid pattern

The most thoroughly documented architecture in this space is the CNN-LSTM model of Sun, Di, Sun, Shen and Lai, "County-Level Soybean Yield Prediction Using Deep CNN-LSTM Model" (peer-reviewed, PMC6832950), full text retrieved directly [14]. The architecture stacks two Conv2D layers (32 and 64 filters, 1×2 kernels, each with batch normalization and 1×2 max-pooling) applied via a TimeDistributed wrapper to every time step of a histogram-based input tensor, followed by a single 256-unit LSTM layer, a 64-unit dense layer, dropout (0.5), and a dense output layer, trained with Adam and early stopping [14]. Inputs were MODIS surface reflectance (7 bands, 500 m), MODIS land surface temperature (day/night, 1 km) and Daymet precipitation/vapor pressure (1 km), resampled to 34±1 eight-day time steps from April to December, aggregated to county level via 32-bin per-band histograms across 15 U.S. states (88.75% of national soybean area), 2003–2015 [14]. At end-of-season (2011–2015 average), CNN-LSTM achieved RMSE = 329.53 kg/ha and R² = 0.78, outperforming a pure CNN (RMSE 359.12, i.e., 8.98% worse) and a pure LSTM (RMSE 363.15, i.e., 10.20% worse); an in-season prediction made on 21 August (well before harvest) reached RMSE = 353.74 kg/ha and R² = 0.74, only about 7% worse than the full-season figure [14]. This is a directly relevant category-1 baseline for HARVEST: it quantifies both the ceiling of a full-season CNN-LSTM (R²=0.78) and the degradation when the trajectory is truncated before the end of season, which is precisely the gap HARVEST's future-trajectory forecasting/reconstruction step (category 4) aims to close. Note that this study uses MODIS, not Sentinel-2, so its spatial and spectral resolution differs substantially from the HARVEST/OGOR Sentinel-2 context; it is included as an architecture-and-degradation-pattern reference rather than a Sentinel-2-specific benchmark.

**Confidence: HIGH | Depth: HIGH**

A structurally similar CNN-LSTM design was also identified for maize in Zambia, "A Deep Learning Model for Corn Yield Prediction Using Spatial and Temporal Features" (ICICT proceedings) [19], which explicitly uses Sentinel-2 vegetation indices (NDVI, EVI) as the CNN's spatial input combined with an LSTM branch over rainfall/temperature/humidity time series. This confirms the CNN(spatial)-LSTM(temporal) pattern recurs specifically with Sentinel-2 inputs, but the accessible content did not include spatial/temporal resolution figures or quantitative R²/RMSE/MAE results, so it cannot be used for quantitative comparison [19].

**Confidence: LOW | Depth: LOW**
**Uncertainty:** [19] confirms architecture and input-modality choice only; accuracy metrics are unverified and the full paper should be consulted before citing any performance number.

### Vision transformer and multimodal attention architectures

CMAViT ("Crop Yield Prediction Model... Integrating Climate, Management, and Remote Sensing Data with Multimodal Vision Transformers," arXiv:2411.16989) represents a more recent transformer-based departure from the CNN-LSTM pattern, using a cross-attention encoder to fuse remote-sensing imagery, short-term weather variation, and text-encoded management practices [15]. Applied to vineyard yield estimation across 2,200 hectares and over 5 million individual vines (2016–2019), CMAViT reached R² = 0.84 and MAPE = 8.22% on held-out test data, outperforming a UNet-ConvLSTM baseline, particularly on extreme (very high/very low) yield values [15]. An ablation confirmed each modality's contribution: removing management-practice text dropped R² to 0.73 (MAPE 11.92%), removing climate data dropped R² to 0.70 (MAPE 12.66%), and removing both dropped R² to 0.72 (MAPE 12.39%) [15]. For HARVEST, CMAViT is relevant as a category-2 architecture (partial/full EO trajectory plus meteorological and management auxiliary data fused via attention) rather than a category-4 pipeline: it fuses observed data streams for a single final prediction and does not forecast or reconstruct an unobserved future trajectory before predicting yield. It is also a different crop system (perennial vineyards, not annual row crops), which limits direct transferability to HARVEST's crop-yield context but is still informative on transformer-based multimodal fusion design.

**Confidence: HIGH | Depth: MEDIUM**

### Synthesis: architecture landscape and relevance to HARVEST category 4

Across the six architecture-specific sources gathered in this pass, a consistent pattern emerges: CNN-LSTM (and its Bi-LSTM variants) is the dominant hybrid design for full-season EO-based yield prediction, with the CNN sub-network extracting per-time-step spatial/spectral features and the LSTM/BiLSTM sub-network integrating them temporally [14][18][19]. [17]'s title indicates an attention-augmented CNN with contrastive learning rather than a confirmed LSTM/BiLSTM component, so it is not grouped with the CNN-LSTM pattern here — its architecture remains unverified (see Uncertainty above) and should not be assumed to match [14][18][19]. Separately, transformer-based multimodal fusion (CMAViT) is an emerging alternative for combining EO with heterogeneous auxiliary data via cross-attention rather than concatenation [15]. The one source with a verified, direct in-season-vs-end-of-season comparison ([14]) shows that truncating the observed trajectory before harvest degrades performance only modestly for a well-optimized CNN-LSTM (R² 0.78 → 0.74, roughly seven weeks before typical harvest), which is an important calibration point for HARVEST: it suggests that any category-4 pipeline (forecast/reconstruct the missing trajectory tail, then predict yield) needs to outperform not just a naive category-1 baseline but a category-1 baseline that already degrades gracefully with partial observation. None of the six sources in this subsection implements or evaluates a category-3 or category-4 pipeline (forecasting or reconstructing the future EO/vegetation trajectory prior to yield prediction) — all six take the observed trajectory, complete or partial, as a direct input to a single end-to-end yield-prediction network. This reinforces, but does not by itself confirm, the novelty gap HARVEST targets; a dedicated search focused specifically on vegetation-index/EO trajectory forecasting methods (e.g., LSTM/Transformer/Gaussian-Process extrapolation of NDVI time series, such as the corn-NDVI-forecasting study referenced in search results but not yet retrieved in this pass) remains necessary to complete the category-3/category-4 gap analysis.

**Confidence: MEDIUM | Depth: MEDIUM**
**Uncertainty:** Of the six architecture-specific sources in this subsection, only two are verified full-text/direct-abstract reads ([14], [15]); the other four ([16], [17], [18], [19]) are unverified leads (title/metadata/snippet only). [12] from the prior subsection is mentioned for context but is not one of the six. The claim that "none of these implement category 4" is accurate for the six sources examined here but is not a comprehensive claim about the literature as a whole — a separate, targeted search for trajectory-forecasting-then-yield-prediction pipelines is required before this can support a novelty claim in the funding proposal.

**Architecture-class gap:** All six sources in this subsection are CNN-LSTM/BiLSTM hybrids or multimodal cross-attention fusion models (CMAViT); none is a pure temporal-transformer/self-attention encoder applied directly to a single EO time series (e.g., a Sentinel-2 pixel- or patch-time-series transformer without CNN or multimodal fusion components, analogous to designs like TSViT or L-TAE in the broader satellite-image-time-series literature). This architecture class was not searched in this pass and should not be assumed absent from the literature — it is a distinct open question from the category-3/category-4 trajectory-forecasting gap noted above and warrants its own targeted search before any claim that "CNN-LSTM is the dominant/best architecture" is used to justify HARVEST's model choice.
