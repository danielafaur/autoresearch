# Auxiliary Information Approaches

This section addresses methodological comparison point 2: yield prediction using partial EO observations **plus** meteorological, historical or crop-model information to compensate for the unobserved future part of the growing season. This is the closest conceptual neighbor to HARVEST's category-4 pipeline, since both attempt to fill the "gap" between the prediction date and harvest — but, as shown below, the mechanism used to fill that gap differs fundamentally from HARVEST's approach of forecasting/reconstructing the EO or vegetation-index trajectory itself.

## Studies Combining Weather Forecasts with Partial EO Observations

Three studies were identified that explicitly combine partial-season Earth Observation data with numerical/seasonal weather forecasts to compensate for the unobserved future part of the crop cycle, all built around **process-based crop growth models** rather than direct EO-trajectory forecasting.

**North China Plain winter wheat — crop-model/LAI assimilation + seasonal weather forecasts.** A study titled "Potential of remote sensing data-crop model assimilation and seasonal weather forecasts for early-season crop yield forecasting over a large area" examined winter wheat across the central North China Plain (2008–2015) [29]. The method assimilates remote-sensing-derived Leaf Area Index (LAI), observed only up to the prediction date, into a process-based crop growth model, and then drives the model forward to harvest using seasonal weather forecasts as meteorological forcing for the unobserved future period, rather than historical climatology or persistence. Reported forecasting skill increases as lead time shortens: at roughly a five-week lead time before harvest, mean MAPE < 10%, ROC score > 0.8, and ACC > 0.65; at roughly a two-month lead time, mean MAPE < 12%, ROC score > 0.75, and ACC > 0.55 [29]. This demonstrates a monotonic degradation of forecast quality with increasing distance from harvest — directly relevant to HARVEST's core question of how prediction accuracy changes with the length of the unobserved future segment.

**Field-scale wheat forecasting — InfoCrop-Wheat + Ensemble Kalman Filter + weather forecast.** "Field scale spatial wheat yield forecasting system under limited field data availability by integrating crop simulation model with weather forecast and satellite remote sensing," published in *Agricultural Systems* (2021), assimilates multidate remotely sensed LAI (retrieved by inversion of a canopy radiative transfer model) into the InfoCrop-Wheat crop simulation model using an Ensemble Kalman Filter, combined with weather forecast data to project phenology, biomass and yield forward to harvest at field scale [30]. The reported result is qualitative but explicit: forecast accuracy improved with each successive date of data assimilation, i.e., accuracy is a monotonic function of how much of the season has already been observed — the same underlying relationship HARVEST investigates, but addressed here through repeated re-assimilation into a mechanistic model rather than through learned trajectory forecasting/reconstruction.

**North China Plain winter wheat — hybrid data assimilation + crop model + machine learning.** A related but distinct study, "Integrating data assimilation, crop model, and machine learning for winter wheat yield forecasting in the North China Plain," adds a machine-learning correction/ensemble layer on top of the assimilation-plus-crop-model pipeline [31]. Aggregated search-index snippets report substantially higher accuracy for this hybrid approach (ACC = 0.97, MAPE = 1.74%) than the crop-model-assimilation-only approach in [29] (ACC > 0.55, MAPE < 12%) — suggesting that adding a machine-learning layer on top of crop-model-plus-weather-forecast assimilation can materially improve on the mechanistic-model-only accuracy gain, though the two studies use different regions/years/validation setups so this is not a controlled comparison.

## Accuracy Gains vs. EO-Only Baseline

None of the three primary studies above report a strictly controlled ablation isolating "EO-only" (no weather forecast, no crop model) against "EO + weather forecast" performance at the *same* prediction date — the closest available evidence is the lead-time degradation pattern in [29] and the assimilation-date progression in [30], both of which show accuracy improving as more of the season is observed and as the unobserved gap (bridged by the weather forecast) shrinks. A more directly comparable ablation number comes from a related (non-weather-forecast) data-assimilation study: assimilating LAI into a sunflower crop growth model reduced RMSE from 9.88 q/ha (open-loop simulation, no assimilation) to 7.49 q/ha (with assimilation), a statistically significant improvement [32]. This figure is included as supporting context for the general principle that constraining a forward crop-model simulation with in-season observations improves on unconstrained simulation, but [32] does not itself include a weather-forecast component distinct from the crop model's standard meteorological forcing, so it should not be read as a direct measurement of the weather-forecast-specific accuracy gain requested by this task's acceptance criteria.

**Uncertainty:** The acceptance criterion "reported accuracy gains vs EO-only baseline captured" is only partially satisfiable from the evidence gathered in this pass. The three core studies report accuracy as a function of lead time or assimilation date, not as a direct before/after comparison against a no-weather-forecast EO-only control. This should be flagged as a genuine evidence gap rather than papered over — a more targeted literature pass (or direct access to the full texts of [29]–[31], e.g., via institutional subscription) would be needed to extract a clean ablation number.

## Comparison to HARVEST's Trajectory-Reconstruction Concept

All three studies in this section address the same underlying problem HARVEST addresses — the future part of the crop trajectory is unobserved at prediction time — but solve it with a **mechanistic, process-based approach**: an explicit crop growth model (InfoCrop-Wheat, or an unnamed process-based model in [29]/[31]) is initialized/constrained by assimilating the *observed* partial EO signal (typically LAI), and is then run forward using weather-forecast meteorological forcing to simulate the physiologically expected future biomass accumulation and, ultimately, yield [29][30][31]. The "future" that is produced is a simulated crop-state trajectory internal to the crop model (e.g., simulated LAI/biomass), not a forecast of the future satellite-observed vegetation-index (e.g., NDVI) time series itself, and it is not produced by a data-driven sequence model (LSTM, Transformer, etc.) of the kind HARVEST's category-3/category-4 pipeline relies on.

This is the key scientific distinction HARVEST must draw against comparison point 2: these crop-model/weather-forecast approaches compensate for the unobserved future by **simulating** it through domain physiology equations driven by forecast weather, whereas HARVEST's category-4 pipeline proposes to **forecast or reconstruct the EO/vegetation-index trajectory itself** using learned temporal models, and then feed that reconstructed trajectory into a yield predictor. The two approaches are complementary rather than identical: a crop-growth-model-plus-weather-forecast approach requires calibrating (or having available) a process-based model with crop-specific physiological parameters and reliable local meteorological forcing/forecast data, while an EO-trajectory-forecasting approach is data-driven and, in principle, agnostic to explicit crop physiology, but has not — in the three studies reviewed here — been evaluated using a data-driven-forecast future EO trajectory as an intermediate step feeding a downstream yield model. No study identified in this pass performs the full HARVEST category-4 sequence (partial EO → forecast/reconstructed future EO trajectory → completed trajectory → yield); the closest prior art uses partial EO → assimilation into a crop model → weather-forecast-driven simulation → yield, which is architecturally and conceptually distinct at the "future trajectory" step. This observation is consistent with (and should be cross-referenced against) the broader gap-analysis findings expected in the dedicated HARVEST Gap Analysis section.
