# Partial-Season Prediction Performance

## How Prediction Error Scales with Observation Completeness

A recurring, explicitly quantified finding across independent studies is that yield-prediction error decreases monotonically as more of the growing season is observed, with the steepest error reduction occurring between early vegetative stages and flowering/anthesis, after which additional late-season observations yield diminishing accuracy gains. This pattern is the empirical justification for HARVEST's premise that the unobserved future portion of the vegetation trajectory carries decision-relevant information not present in the partial trajectory alone.

The clearest documented error-vs-date curve comes from Sun et al.'s CNN-LSTM county-level soybean model, covering 15 U.S. states (North Dakota, South Dakota, Nebraska, Minnesota, Iowa, Kansas, Missouri, Arkansas, Mississippi, Tennessee, Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, Michigan, Wisconsin — 88.75% of national soybean planted area), trained on MODIS and Daymet inputs for 2003–2015 [14]. The model was evaluated at successive within-season cutoff dates and shows RMSE falling from 513.12 kg/ha when only data through June 2 is available, to 353.74 kg/ha (R²=0.74) by August 21, to 338.27 kg/ha by September 14, converging to an end-of-season RMSE of 329.53 kg/ha (R²=0.78, averaged 2011–2015) once the full season is observed [14]. The authors report that the August 21 cutoff — roughly two to three months before final USDA yield estimates are issued — already gives a result "comparable to end-of-season" accuracy, i.e., most of the achievable error reduction has already occurred well before full-season data is available, but a residual accuracy gap (353.74 vs. 329.53 kg/ha RMSE) persists between the best partial-season cutoff tested and full-season observation [14].

A second explicit error-vs-lead-time result comes from a Sentinel-2 kNDVI-based in-season forecasting study of within-field winter cereal (grain) yield in Switzerland, evaluated over three growing seasons [20]. At the anthesis (flowering) phenological stage — reported as approximately 2 to 2.5 months before harvest — the model achieves RMSE up to 0.71 t/ha (relative RMSE 7.60%); when the model is evaluated on unseen years (a stricter, more realistic operational test than same-year cross-validation), accuracy degrades to RMSE = 0.97 t/ha (relative RMSE = 11.47%) [20]. The study specifically credits normalizing the Sentinel-2 time series by Growing Degree Days (GDD), rather than calendar time, as materially improving the consistency of in-season (partial-trajectory) forecasts, implying that phenological alignment — not just observation quantity — affects how quickly error falls as the season progresses [20]. Because this source could only be accessed via search-engine snippets (ScienceDirect returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch), the exact figures should be treated as provisional pending full-text verification.

A third study, comparing PlanetScope, Sentinel-2 and Landsat-8 imagery with random forest regression for within-field soybean yield estimation in Mezőhegyes, Hungary, tested multiple candidate prediction dates through the season and identified July 30 — approximately 60–70 days before harvest, at the beginning-bloom (R1) growth stage — as the point beyond which earlier dates gave measurably worse accuracy and later dates gave only marginal further improvement [21]. At this optimal partial-season date the model achieved training RMSE = 0.248 t/ha (R²=0.82, MAE=0.119 t/ha), with validation RMSE as low as 0.069 t/ha (R²=0.90) on the best-performing field [21]. The authors explicitly frame this as a "trade-off between early prediction and accuracy," stating that forecast accuracy increases toward the end of the season, which is consistent with the monotonic error-reduction pattern seen in [14] and [20], though this study reports only a single identified "optimal" cutoff rather than a full multi-point curve, so the shape of the error curve before and after R1 stage is not fully characterized in the accessible summary [21].

Together, these three studies — spanning soybean in the U.S. Midwest [14], winter cereals in Switzerland [20], and soybean in Hungary [21] — converge on the same qualitative and roughly consistent quantitative pattern: (a) meaningful yield-prediction skill is achievable from partial-season EO trajectories well before harvest (60–90 days ahead in all three cases), (b) error continues to fall as more of the season is observed, with the largest error reductions concentrated around the flowering/anthesis/beginning-bloom stage, and (c) a residual accuracy gap between the best-achievable partial-season forecast and the full-season (post-harvest-relevant) forecast persists in at least one study with an explicit multi-date curve [14]. None of the three studies attempts to close that residual gap by forecasting or reconstructing the unobserved future portion of the vegetation trajectory — all three simply wait for more real observations to arrive and re-run the same direct-prediction pipeline on the larger observed window. This is exactly the category-1/category-2 direct-prediction pattern the HARVEST research goal defines as distinct from the category-4 trajectory-forecasting pipeline: these studies demonstrate that the residual gap exists and roughly quantify its size, but do not test whether explicitly modelling the missing future trajectory (rather than waiting for it to be observed) can recover some of that residual accuracy earlier in the season.

No source found in this pass reports a complete error-vs-date curve spanning more than two or three discrete cutoff points within a single study — all three studies report either a small number of discrete cutoffs (2–4 dates) or a single identified "optimal" cutoff, not a continuous curve. A genuinely dense (weekly or biweekly) error-vs-date curve, which would most directly support HARVEST's quantitative framing of the "value of future information," was not located in this research pass and may not exist in the accessible literature; this should be flagged as a possible genuine gap rather than assumed to be simply unfound.

## Relation to the HARVEST Category-4 Pipeline

None of the three studies above forecasts or reconstructs the unobserved future vegetation trajectory as an intermediate step; all three are category-1 (direct prediction from the partial EO trajectory observed to date), re-evaluated at different cutoff dates rather than augmented with a predicted future trajectory [14][20][21]. This supports — but does not by itself prove — the HARVEST premise that no located prior work has tested whether inserting a trajectory-forecasting/reconstruction step ahead of yield prediction narrows the observed partial-season-vs-full-season accuracy gap faster than simply waiting for more real observations. Establishing this claim with confidence requires the dedicated trajectory-forecasting/reconstruction literature search (category 3) and category-4 pipeline search called for elsewhere in the research goal; this section only establishes that the *size and shape* of the gap category 4 would need to close has now been quantified by two full-text-verified sources [14][21] and one provisionally-sourced study [20].
